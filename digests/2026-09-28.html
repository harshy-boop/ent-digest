The Impact of Surgical Technique on Post-Tonsillectomy Hemorrhage — A Registry-Based Cohort Study

Zahl SM, Bugten V, Myklebust TÅ, et al.

BACKGROUND

Post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage (PTH) remains the most feared tonsillectomy complication. While observational evidence has long suggested thermal techniques carry higher hemorrhage risk, population-level comparative data with proper confounding adjustment were lacking.

METHODS

Registry-based cohort of all Norwegian tonsillectomies (excluding tonsillotomies) 2017–2025 (n=25,953). Surgical technique classified as cold, cold+warm (thermal hemostasis only), or warm (thermal dissection and hemostasis). Logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and indication.

RESULTS

PTH occurred in 7.5% overall. Cold technique (n=3,348) was lowest-risk; cold+warm (n=16,284) OR 2.4 (95%CI 1.9–3.1); warm (n=6,321) OR 3.4 (95%CI 2.5–4.6). Absolute risk increase: 4.5% and 7.5%, respectively. NNH 22 and 13. Risk elevation most pronounced in adolescents and adults.

LIMITATIONS

Retrospective registry; technique classification relied on administrative coding. Surgeon volume and institutional protocols not captured. No data on hematoma severity or reoperation rates by technique.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

Cold tonsillectomy technique confers substantially lower PTH risk. For adolescent and adult patients especially, the evidence now supports a practice-level recommendation toward cold technique — an NNH of 13 for warm technique is a compelling safety signal.

Why score 4: Population-level registry covering ALL tonsillectomies in Norway 2017–2025 (n=25,953). NNH of 13 for fully warm vs cold technique directly quantifies risk, with a clear dose-response (cold+warm OR 2.4, warm OR 3.4). Effect is most pronounced in adolescents/adults — directly actionable for surgeons choosing technique.

Low Arousal Threshold and Long-Term Adherence to Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation

Kaki PC, Ravuri S, Patel SD, et al.

BACKGROUND

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HGNS/Inspire) has grown rapidly as a CPAP-alternative for OSA. Low arousal threshold (ArTH) is a distinct OSA endotype previously associated with CPAP non-adherence, but its effect on HGNS adherence was unknown.

METHODS

Retrospective cohort at Univ. of Pittsburgh and Thomas Jefferson Univ. (n=160). Low vs high ArTH estimated preoperatively using a validated composite scoring algorithm on routine sleep study data. Primary outcome: HGNS adherence (≥4 h/night on >70% of nights) at 3 and 6 months post-activation.

RESULTS

41% had low ArTH. Low ArTH patients transitioned from adherent at 3 months to non-adherent at 6 months at a 5-fold higher rate (26% vs 5.3%; OR 12.60, 95%CI 3.70–56.10). Despite lower baseline AHI, low ArTH patients had smaller relative AHI reductions post-HGNS.

LIMITATIONS

Retrospective, n=160, short follow-up (6 months). ArTH algorithm is a validated surrogate, not physiologically measured. Potential referral bias at 2 academic centers.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

Low ArTH — estimable from any preoperative polysomnogram — predicts progressive therapy disengagement from HGNS, not nightly intolerance but missed nights. Routine ArTH endotyping pre-HGNS candidacy evaluation is now supported; high-risk patients warrant intensified post-activation follow-up and device programming strategies.

Why score 4: HGNS is the fastest-growing surgical OSA treatment; understanding who disengages from therapy is high-value. JAMA Otolaryngol flagship. N=160 across 2 academic centers with objective adherence data. OR 12.6 for drop-off in low vs high arousal threshold group is a strong, actionable signal for preoperative counseling.

Hormone Replacement Therapy and Recurrence Risk in Postmenopausal Idiopathic Subglottic Stenosis

Awadallah AS, Fearington FW, Ali HM, et al.

BACKGROUND

Idiopathic subglottic stenosis (iSGS) is a fibroinflammatory condition affecting almost exclusively perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, suggesting hormonal involvement. The effect of systemic hormone replacement therapy (HRT) on iSGS recurrence has not previously been examined.

METHODS

Retrospective cohort at Mayo Clinic (2002–2024) of 125 postmenopausal women (≥50 years) undergoing laser-wedge excision for iSGS. Patients categorized by HRT exposure at or before first surgery (never, past, current, subsequent). Negative binomial regression adjusted for age, BMI, parity, oophorectomy, and SILSI use.

RESULTS

96 never-HRT, 29 ever-HRT (including 19 current users). Current HRT use: 53% lower adjusted recurrence rate vs never-HRT (IRR 0.47, 95%CI 0.25–0.89, p=0.02). Surgery-free intervals and time to first recurrence trended longer in current users but did not reach significance.

LIMITATIONS

Retrospective, single institution, n=125. HRT type, dose, and duration not fully characterized. Cannot exclude confounding by unmeasured hormonal variables. Temporality and causality cannot be established.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

Current systemic HRT at iSGS presentation is associated with a significant reduction in procedural recurrence. These findings support incorporating HRT status into iSGS management discussions — for postmenopausal patients considering HRT for other indications, its potential airway benefit is now evidence-supported.

Why score 4: Idiopathic subglottic stenosis affects almost exclusively postmenopausal women, and the hormonal hypothesis has long been suspected but untested. Mayo Clinic with 22-year follow-up, 125 patients. Current HRT use → 53% lower recurrence rate (IRR 0.47, p=0.02) — a clinically meaningful effect that directly informs shared decision-making in this challenging condition.

Efficacy of Endoscopic Sinus Surgery for Allergic Fungal Rhinosinusitis: A Systematic Review With Meta-Analysis

Kais A, Kfouri J, Sedaghat AR, et al.

BACKGROUND

Allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS) is an eosinophilic-driven form of CRS for which ESS is the cornerstone treatment, yet outcomes vary and postoperative management is unstandardized — particularly regarding antifungal therapy.

METHODS

PRISMA-compliant systematic review of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane from inception to October 2025. Included studies reporting outcomes of ESS in AFRS. Meta-analysis of recurrence rate, revision rate, and SNOT-22 change. 31 studies, 1,474 patients; mean follow-up 13.7 months.

RESULTS

Pooled disease recurrence rate: 31% (95%CI 25–38%). Pooled revision surgery rate: 19% (95%CI 14–25%). ESS significantly improved SNOT-22 by 23.4 points (95%CI 18.7–28.0). Oral itraconazole did NOT reduce recurrence (30.7% vs 31.1%, p=0.957) nor revision rate (18.2% vs 20.3%, p=0.643).

LIMITATIONS

High heterogeneity in follow-up duration (1.5–32.5 months) and postoperative regimens limits direct comparison. Most included studies are retrospective with small sample sizes. Publication bias cannot be excluded. AFRS diagnostic criteria varied across studies.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

ESS for AFRS provides meaningful QOL improvement but expects a 1-in-3 recurrence rate. Oral itraconazole does not reduce recurrence, challenging a common postoperative practice and pointing toward immunomodulatory or biologic strategies rather than routine antifungal prophylaxis.

Why score 4: Systematic review with meta-analysis of 31 studies across 1,474 AFRS patients in the flagship Otolaryngol HNS journal. Quantifies overall recurrence (31%) and revision surgery (19%) rates with confidence intervals, and critically finds that oral itraconazole does not reduce recurrence — directly relevant to rhinology practice.

When to Use the HINTS Examination in Patients With Dizziness: A Review

Sars C, Lelli D, Tse D, et al.

BACKGROUND

The HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) was introduced in 2009 to bedside-distinguish peripheral vestibular disorders from central causes such as stroke in acute vestibular syndrome (AVS). Despite wide adoption, misapplication rates remain high and multiple guidelines have introduced contradictory or ambiguous recommendations.

METHODS

Narrative review synthesizing HINTS validation literature, guideline evolution (including GRACE-3), and common misapplication patterns. Examines performance data stratified by correct vs incorrect HINTS application criteria.

RESULTS

HINTS maintains high stroke sensitivity ONLY when applied to patients with continuous dizziness AND spontaneous nystagmus at rest (true AVS). Common errors: applying HINTS to patients without nystagmus, failing to screen for central features first, misinterpreting the head impulse test. GRACE-3 introduced ambiguity around pre-HINTS screening that may worsen clinical decision-making.

LIMITATIONS

Narrative rather than systematic review. Quantitative sensitivity/specificity data depend on heterogeneous included studies. Recommendations for guideline revision have not yet been endorsed.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

HINTS should be performed ONLY in patients with continuous dizziness and spontaneous nystagmus at rest — screening for central features (ataxia, diplopia, headache) must precede the exam. Applied correctly: sensitivity for stroke >96%. Applied incorrectly: sensitivity collapses. Share these criteria with your ED colleagues — this is a system-level safety issue.

Why score 4: Published in JAMA Neurology (top-tier), this review clarifies a pervasive diagnostic error: HINTS misapplication in patients without spontaneous nystagmus. Confusion around GRACE-3 guidelines is explicitly addressed. Relevant to every ENT and neurotology provider seeing acute dizziness — proper HINTS use has high sensitivity for stroke when correctly applied but declines sharply with misuse.
