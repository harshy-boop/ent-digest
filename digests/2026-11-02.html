Clinical Review

Diagnosis and Management of Acute Rhinosinusitis in Adults.

Fan J, Alexander JT … Alexander JT (senior, Department of Medicine, University of Chicago, Chicago, Illinois)

BACKGROUND

Acute rhinosinusitis (ARS) affects roughly 31 million Americans annually and is the fifth most common diagnosis prompting antibiotic prescriptions, yet most cases are viral and self-limited. Antibiotic overuse in ARS is a major driver of antimicrobial resistance, making evidence-based diagnosis and treatment guidelines critical for ENT and primary care clinicians alike.

METHODS

This JAMA clinical practice review synthesizes current evidence and major guideline recommendations (AAO-HNS, IDSA, Cochrane) on the diagnosis and management of uncomplicated ARS in adults. It covers clinical criteria for distinguishing viral from bacterial etiologies, imaging indications, and treatment hierarchies.

RESULTS

Bacterial ARS is defined by symptom duration >10 days, severe symptoms for ≥3–4 days, or classic 'double worsening.' Amoxicillin-clavulanate remains first-line when antibiotics are indicated; watchful waiting is appropriate for mild-to-moderate presentations meeting bacterial criteria. Intranasal corticosteroids modestly reduce symptom duration as monotherapy; analgesics and saline irrigation are recommended for all patients. Imaging is reserved for suspected complications or diagnostic uncertainty.

LIMITATIONS

Many supporting trials are heterogeneous in definitions and populations. The review does not address chronic or recurrent ARS, immunocompromised hosts, or complicated ARS with orbital/intracranial extension. Evidence on decongestants and antihistamines remains weak.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

For ENT clinicians: apply the 10-day / severe-symptom / double-worsening criteria to gate antibiotic use; prefer amoxicillin-clavulanate when antibiotics are indicated; recommend intranasal steroids plus saline for all patients; reserve imaging for suspected complications. Watchful waiting can be offered to reliable patients with mild bacterial presentations.

Why score 5: JAMA clinical practice review on the most common outpatient antibiotic indication — directly shapes prescribing and management decisions for every ENT. Top-tier journal, high generalizability.

Videolaryngoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy for nasotracheal intubation in infants undergoing cardiac procedures (NasoVISI): a multicentre, randomised controlled trial.

Garcia-Marcinkiewicz AG, Gottlieb EA, Twite M et al. … Nicolson SC (senior, The Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania)

BACKGROUND

Nasotracheal intubation (NTI) is routinely required for infants undergoing cardiac or major head/neck procedures. While videolaryngoscopy (VL) improves first-attempt success for orotracheal intubation in infants, its advantage for the technically distinct NTI route was previously untested in a definitive RCT.

METHODS

Multicenter, parallel-group RCT across 8 US pediatric cardiac centers. Infants ≤365 days without anticipated difficult airways requiring NTI for elective cardiothoracic surgery or catheterization were randomized 1:1 to VL or direct laryngoscopy (DL). Primary outcome: first-attempt success rate. Modified intention-to-treat with generalized estimating equations accounting for clustering by intubating clinician.

RESULTS

673 infants analyzed (median age 14 weeks, mean weight 4.77 kg). First-attempt success: 83% (VL) vs 75% (DL); adjusted risk difference +8.2% (95% CI 2.0–14.3%, p=0.009). Per-attempt and cumulative complication rates were similar (~13–14%) between groups. No cardiac arrests or deaths in either group.

LIMITATIONS

Trial conducted at specialist pediatric cardiac centers by experienced intubators, limiting generalizability to community or urgent settings. Infants with anticipated difficult airways were excluded. Device heterogeneity across sites and lack of long-term outcomes are additional limitations.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

For ENT surgeons and anesthesiologists intubating infants nasally: VL achieves ~8% absolute improvement in first-attempt success over DL with no increase in complications. This extends VL's advantage to the nasotracheal route and supports VL as the preferred technique for NTI in infants in specialist settings.

Why score 4: Multicenter RCT (n=673, 8 centers) in Lancet Child & Adolescent Health — strong methodology, directly relevant to ENT physicians and anesthesiologists managing infant airways. First-attempt success rate is an important safety metric.

Nasal Obstruction and Treatment With Oxymetazoline and Corticosteroids: A Randomized Clinical Trial.

Cruz L, Rammell J, Kallogjeri D et al. … Farrell NF (senior, Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Washington University School of Medicine, St Louis, Missouri)

BACKGROUND

Oxymetazoline is among the most effective nasal decongestants, but chronic use is avoided due to rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa). Prior evidence suggested intranasal corticosteroids might prevent tachyphylaxis, potentially enabling long-term combination use. This question is highly relevant to patients with chronic nasal obstruction from rhinitis who have failed steroids alone.

METHODS

Single-center double-blind RCT (Washington University/Barnes Jewish, Oct 2024–Jul 2025). Adults with chronic nasal obstruction, prior topical steroid failure, and no chronic sinusitis. Randomized to oxymetazoline/budesonide (n=31) vs budesonide alone (n=29) nasal spray BID for 4 weeks, then 3-week wean. Primary outcome: ≥moderate improvement on CGI-I at 4 weeks. Subset (n=10) had pre/post inferior turbinate biopsies.

RESULTS

Responder rate: 83% (oxymetazoline/budesonide) vs 36% (budesonide alone), difference 47 percentage points (95% CI 25–70 pp). No participants in the combination arm developed rebound obstruction during 4-week treatment. All successfully weaned off oxymetazoline without difficulty. Histopathology data pending.

LIMITATIONS

Single center, short treatment duration (4 weeks), modest sample size (n=60). Histopathological outcomes from the biopsy cohort are not yet reported. Longer follow-up is needed to assess rebound risk beyond 4 weeks and safety of indefinite use. The wean protocol was investigator-directed, not fully standardized.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

For rhinologists managing chronic nasal obstruction: combined oxymetazoline/budesonide nearly triples the responder rate vs budesonide alone over 4 weeks, without inducing rhinitis medicamentosa in this timeframe. This regimen may be offered to patients failing topical steroids, with informed discussion of uncertainty around longer-term use. A prescription-strength fixed-dose combination product is not yet available.

Why score 4: Double-blind RCT in JAMA Otolaryngology addressing a common high-stakes clinical question: can oxymetazoline be used long-term with steroids without inducing rhinitis medicamentosa? The 47-percentage-point difference in responder rates is clinically significant.

Tezepelumab in Canadian Adults With Severe Chronic Rhinosinusitis With Nasal Polyps: A Subgroup Analysis of the Phase 3 WAYPOINT Trial.

Desrosiers M, Corriveau MN, Gagnon R et al. … Sowerby LJ (senior, Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Western University, London, ON, Canada)

BACKGROUND

Tezepelumab, an anti-TSLP monoclonal antibody, was recently approved in Canada for severe uncontrolled CRSwNP. The Canadian real-world patient profile — with high asthma and NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease (NSAID-ERD) comorbidity — differs meaningfully from international trial populations, raising the question of whether trial results translate.

METHODS

Post hoc subgroup analysis of 49 Canadian patients from the multinational WAYPOINT Phase 3 RCT. Adults with severe uncontrolled CRSwNP randomized to tezepelumab 210 mg SC Q4W (n=25) or placebo (n=24) for 52 weeks. Co-primary endpoints: change from baseline in total nasal polyp score (NPS) and biweekly nasal congestion score (NCS).

RESULTS

Both co-primary endpoints significantly improved with tezepelumab vs placebo (NPS LS mean difference –2.53 [95% CI –3.33 to –1.73]; NCS statistically significant as well). The Canadian cohort had higher rates of asthma and NSAID-ERD than the global population, and effect sizes were maintained. Safety profile was consistent with the full trial.

LIMITATIONS

Post hoc subgroup analysis (n=49); underpowered for formal statistical testing of interaction effects. Canadian-specific context may not generalize to other health systems. Small sample limits precision of effect estimates.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

For rhinologists considering biologics in CRSwNP: tezepelumab shows consistent efficacy even in patients with high asthma and NSAID-ERD burden — a profile common in Canadian and US tertiary practices. For complex CRSwNP patients who have failed or are not candidates for dupilumab, tezepelumab is a clinically supported option. Access via prior authorization pathways will remain the rate-limiting step.

Why score 4: Phase 3 RCT data (subgroup) confirming tezepelumab efficacy in CRSwNP in a clinically realistic population (high asthma/NSAID-ERD comorbidity); relevant as tezepelumab is now approved in Canada and likely to gain broader approval. Supports prescribing in complex multimorbid patients.

Limosilactobacillus reuteri for oral mucositis in patients undergoing radiotherapy for malignant head and neck tumors: a randomized controlled trial.

Gao K, Li YT, Pei YY et al. … Peng XC (senior, Department of Biotherapy, Cancer Center, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, Chengdu, Sichuan, China)

BACKGROUND

Oral mucositis (OM) affects the majority of patients undergoing definitive radiotherapy for head and neck cancer and is a leading cause of treatment interruption, hospitalization, and reduced quality of life. Severe OM (WHO grade ≥3) currently has no approved preventive therapy; standard care is palifermin (for chemo-conditioning) and topical analgesics. Microbiome interventions offer a low-risk preventive strategy.

METHODS

Double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 RCT (NCT06285591) at West China Hospital, Mar 2024–Jan 2025. 160 H&N cancer patients randomized 1:1 to L. reuteri probiotic lozenges vs identical placebo twice daily throughout radiotherapy. Primary endpoint: incidence of severe oral mucositis (WHO grade ≥3). Secondary: OM onset, duration, any-grade incidence.

RESULTS

Primary endpoint met: SOM incidence 37.5% (L. reuteri) vs 61.3% (placebo); RR 0.61 (95% CI 0.44–0.85), ARR 23.8 pp (95% CI 8.7–38.8 pp), p=0.004. L. reuteri also delayed SOM onset and shortened SOM duration. Any-grade OM incidence was also reduced. No significant safety concerns.

LIMITATIONS

Phase 2 single-center trial; needs Phase 3 replication across heterogeneous radiation protocols and H&N cancer subsites. The specific L. reuteri strain (DSM 17938) is not universally available. Long-term microbiome effects and impact on oncologic outcomes were not assessed.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY

For head and neck oncologic surgeons and radiation oncologists: add L. reuteri probiotic lozenges to the supportive care protocol for patients undergoing H&N RT — the ARR of ~24 percentage points for severe OM is clinically meaningful, the intervention is low-cost, well-tolerated, and can be initiated at radiotherapy start. Confirm availability of DSM 17938 strain. Await Phase 3 confirmation before changing institutional standard-of-care.

Why score 4: Phase 2 double-blind RCT (n=160) in Nature Communications demonstrating a 40% relative risk reduction in severe oral mucositis with a simple probiotic lozenge during H&N cancer radiotherapy. Clinically meaningful ARR of 23.8 pp and no safety concerns; low barrier to implementation.

Prone positioning and high-frequency oscillatory ventilation in early paediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PROSpect): an international, two-by-two factorial, response-adaptive, phase 3, randomised controlled trial.

PROSpect enrolled 424 children with moderate-to-severe PARDS across 75 PICUs in 18 countries. Neither prone positioning nor HFOV improved ventilator-free days compared with supine and conventional ventilation. The trial was stopped early for futility. Prone positioning increased airway obstruction and pressure injuries; HFOV increased hemodynamic instability. Practice implication for ENT: do not recommend prone or HFOV as routine rescue strategies when managing tracheotomized or post-op patients in PICU settings.
