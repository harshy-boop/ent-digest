## Abstract

**Objective.** To map the extent, range, and nature of the evidence on hypoglossal
nerve stimulation (HNS) for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in individuals with Down
syndrome (DS), and to identify gaps to inform future primary research.

**Introduction.** OSA affects the majority of individuals with DS and frequently
persists after adenotonsillectomy. Positive airway pressure (PAP) is poorly tolerated
in this population, leaving few options for refractory disease. HNS, an implantable
upper-airway stimulation therapy, received US FDA approval in March 2023 for
adolescents ≥13 years with DS and persistent OSA. As the treated population grows, a
map of the current evidence — and its gaps — is needed.

**Inclusion criteria.** Studies of any design reporting on HNS/upper-airway
stimulation in individuals of any age with DS and OSA, addressing efficacy,
adherence, safety, patient-reported/neurocognitive outcomes, candidacy/access,
surgical technique, or cost.

**Methods.** A scoping review conducted per the JBI methodology and reported using
PRISMA-ScR. MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, and Scopus
will be searched from inception, supplemented by conference proceedings and reference
screening. Two reviewers will independently screen and chart data. Findings are
summarized descriptively and thematically.

**Results (preliminary).** The identified literature is dominated by single-arm
prospective cohorts, retrospective cohorts, and case reports from a small number of
centers. Evidence consistently reports large reductions in apnea–hypopnea index (AHI)
and high device adherence, but is sparse on controlled comparisons, cardiopulmonary
outcomes, neurocognitive/behavioral outcomes, longer-term safety, and equity of
access.

**Conclusions.** HNS appears efficacious for AHI reduction in carefully selected
individuals with DS, but the evidence base is early-stage and surrogate-focused.
Controlled, beyond-AHI, and longer-term outcome studies are the principal gaps.

## Introduction

### Rationale

Obstructive sleep apnea is highly prevalent in Down syndrome, with reported rates far
exceeding those of the general pediatric population, driven by midface and mandibular
hypoplasia, relative macroglossia, glossoptosis, generalized hypotonia, obesity, and
adenotonsillar hypertrophy. Adenotonsillectomy is the usual first-line surgical
therapy but leaves a large fraction of children with DS with residual OSA. Continuous
positive airway pressure (PAP) is effective when used but is poorly tolerated in this
population, and other surgical options are limited.

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation delivers respiration-synchronized stimulation to the
hypoglossal nerve, producing tongue protrusion that relieves retroglossal collapse.
After establishing efficacy in adults through the STAR trial, a single-arm multicenter
trial extended the therapy to adolescents with DS, culminating in US FDA approval in
March 2023 for individuals with DS aged ≥13 years with severe residual OSA who are
PAP-intolerant [1][6]. First described in a pediatric DS patient in 2016 [7], the
treated population is now expanding across implanting centers.

Because the field is young, heterogeneous in design, and concentrated in a few
programs, the evidence is not yet suited to a question-specific systematic review or
meta-analysis. A scoping review is the appropriate method to map what has been
studied, characterize the designs and outcomes reported, and make the evidence gaps
explicit — an approach an earlier scoping review began to articulate for this exact
topic [16].

### Review objective and questions

**Objective:** to systematically map the evidence on HNS for OSA in individuals with
DS.

**Primary question:** What is the extent, range, and nature of evidence regarding the
efficacy, adherence, safety, and broader outcomes of HNS in individuals with DS and
OSA?

**Sub-questions:**
1. What study designs, populations (age ranges), and settings have been reported?
2. What outcomes have been measured (polysomnographic, patient-reported,
   neurocognitive/behavioral, cardiopulmonary, safety, utilization, economic)?
3. How are candidacy, patient selection, and access described?
4. What surgical and device-titration considerations are specific to DS?
5. What evidence gaps and future research priorities emerge?

## Methods

This review follows the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and is reported using the
**PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)**. The a priori protocol should be
registered (e.g., Open Science Framework) and any amendments documented.

### Eligibility criteria (PCC framework)

| Element | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Individuals of any age with Down syndrome (trisomy 21) and a diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea. |
| **Concept** | Hypoglossal nerve stimulation / upper-airway stimulation (e.g., Inspire), including candidacy evaluation, drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) for selection, implantation, activation/titration, efficacy, adherence, safety, and downstream outcomes. |
| **Context** | Any care setting (tertiary/quaternary sleep-surgery programs, pediatric and adult), any country, any year. |

**Types of sources:** All study designs — RCTs, cohort (prospective/retrospective),
case-control, cross-sectional, case series, case reports, economic evaluations,
guidelines, and conference abstracts. Narrative reviews will be used for
context/reference-mining but charted separately from primary evidence. Non-English
records will be flagged and handled per protocol (translation vs. documented
exclusion).

**Exclusions:** Studies of HNS in populations without DS that do not report
DS-specific data; non-OSA indications; editorials without primary or synthesized data.

### Information sources

MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science Core Collection, and
Scopus, from inception to the search date. Supplementary sources: reference lists of
included studies and relevant reviews; conference proceedings (e.g., *SLEEP*,
AAO-HNSF, ATS); ClinicalTrials.gov; and manufacturer/registry reports. The search will
be re-run before final synthesis to capture new records.

### Search strategy (template — to be finalized with an information specialist)

The strategy will be translated for each database (controlled vocabulary + free text),
peer-reviewed with the **PRESS** checklist, and the full strategy for every source
reported in an appendix with dates and hit counts.

### Selection of sources of evidence

Records will be de-duplicated and screened in two stages (title/abstract, then
full-text) by **two independent reviewers**, with disagreements resolved by discussion
or a third reviewer. Screening will be managed in Covidence or Rayyan. The selection
process will be depicted in a **PRISMA-ScR flow diagram** with reasons for full-text
exclusions.

### Data charting

Two reviewers will independently chart data into a piloted form; discrepancies
reconciled by consensus. Consistent with scoping-review methodology, formal
risk-of-bias/quality appraisal is **not** performed, though study design is recorded.

**Data items:** citation; country/center(s); design; sample size; age range and
mean/median; DS confirmation; OSA severity and diagnostic criteria; prior treatments
(adenotonsillectomy, PAP, other); candidacy criteria and DISE use; device and surgical
technique; activation/titration protocol; **outcomes** — polysomnographic (AHI, ODI,
oxygen nadir, hypoxemia/hypoventilation metrics), adherence (usage hours), patient-
reported (OSA-18/QoL), neurocognitive/behavioral, cardiopulmonary, healthcare
utilization, economic; **safety/adverse events**; follow-up duration; and stated
limitations/future directions.

### Synthesis

Findings will be summarized descriptively (counts, ranges) and organized into thematic
categories (below). Results will be presented in tabular and narrative form, with an
evidence-gap map highlighting under-studied population × outcome cells.

### Thematic synthesis

**1. Efficacy (polysomnographic).** The dominant and most consistent finding: large
AHI reductions across designs and ages — ~85% in the pivotal adolescent series [1],
86–100% titrated-AHI reduction in early adult cases [17], ~76% in adults [14], and
durable response (~88% at ~4 years) in long-term follow-up [6]. Nearly all efficacy
evidence is single-arm.

**2. Adherence.** Reported nightly usage is high (often ~8–9 h/night) [1][14],
notably exceeding typical PAP adherence in DS — a recurring rationale for the therapy.

**3. Beyond-AHI response and non-responders.** A distinct thread argues AHI alone
under-captures benefit: patients with residual elevated AHI still show QoL and
secondary polysomnographic gains [9], and DS-specific titration (sensing-lead
optimization, DISE-directed) can rescue apparent non-responders [3]. This mirrors the
broader move toward physiologic metrics (e.g., hypoxic burden) in the adult HNS
literature.

**4. Neurocognitive / behavioral outcomes.** Identified as the mechanism of greatest
interest but supported only by preliminary data [5]; a clear gap.

**5. Safety and adverse events.** Serious complications appear uncommon in the
efficacy cohorts (occasional revision surgery [1]), but DS-specific safety signals
emerge from case reports: transient post-implant hypoventilation [18] and Down
syndrome regression disorder complicating the peri-implant course [10]. Systematic,
prospective safety capture is limited.

**6. Special populations.** Evidence extends to adults with DS [14][17], younger
children off-label [4], and across BMI categories (obesity did not attenuate AHI
response) [11] — but each rests on small samples.

**7. Candidacy, selection, and access.** A single-program funnel shows how few
screened DS patients ultimately meet manufacturer criteria and proceed (8 eligible and
2 implanted of 1,326 screened) [12], surfacing access/equity and selection questions
that the efficacy literature does not address.

**8. Surgical technique and economics.** Pediatric surgical modifications are
described [15], and modeling suggests cost-effectiveness, possibly below the current
approved age [2].

## Discussion

### Summary of evidence

The literature establishes, with reasonable consistency, that HNS produces large and
durable reductions in AHI with high adherence in carefully selected individuals with
DS and PAP-intolerant residual OSA [1][6][14][17]. However, the evidence is
**early-stage, surrogate-dominated, single-arm, and concentrated in a few centers.**
Efficacy is defined almost entirely by AHI, even as voices within the field argue AHI
under-represents benefit [9].

### Evidence gaps

1. **Controlled comparisons.** Almost no studies include a candidate-but-untreated or
   active-comparator group; causal inference about downstream health is therefore
   limited.
2. **Beyond-AHI physiologic outcomes.** Hypoxic burden and other overnight-hypoxemia
   metrics are essentially unstudied in DS despite their relevance to sequelae.
3. **Cardiopulmonary outcomes.** Pulmonary hypertension and cardiac endpoints — central
   to DS morbidity — are not systematically reported.
4. **Neurocognitive/behavioral outcomes.** The outcomes most valued by patients and
   families remain preliminary [5].
5. **Longer-term and systematic safety.** DS-specific signals (hypoventilation,
   regression disorder) [10][18] need prospective, structured surveillance.
6. **Access and equity.** The candidacy funnel and disparities in who reaches implant
   are barely characterized [12].
7. **Younger children and device optimization.** Off-label younger implantation [4]
   and titration strategy [3] need dedicated study.

### Implications for research

These gaps define a tractable agenda for a well-phenotyped, single-center cohort with
both a large DS denominator and an implanting program. Priorities that require rich
clinical data rather than large N: (a) a **candidacy funnel** quantifying the eligible-
but-not-implanted comparator [12]; (b) **beyond-AHI physiology**, applying hypoxic-
burden analysis to DS PSGs [9]; and (c) **cardiopulmonary outcomes** using serial
echocardiography, with congenital heart disease pre-specified as an effect modifier.
Neurocognitive/behavioral outcomes [5] are the natural prospective extension.

### Limitations of this review

As a scoping review, no formal quality appraisal was undertaken; findings describe the
*presence* and *shape* of evidence, not its validity. A substantial share of the
current evidence is in conference-abstract form [10][12][18], which may change on full
publication. Restriction to indexed databases and English-language records (per
protocol) may miss records. **Most importantly, the counts and inclusion set in this
draft are provisional pending execution of the full, documented search.**

---

## Conclusion

HNS is an efficacious therapy for AHI reduction with high adherence in selected
individuals with Down syndrome and refractory, PAP-intolerant OSA, and its use is
expanding since 2023 FDA approval. The evidence base, however, is dominated by
single-arm, AHI-focused, single-center reports. Controlled designs and outcomes beyond
AHI — physiologic (hypoxic burden), cardiopulmonary, neurocognitive/behavioral, safety,
and access — are the defining gaps and the clearest priorities for future primary
research.
